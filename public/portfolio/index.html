Research synthesis

What the product needed to solve

F01
Important information was fragmented.

Parents of children with special needs need child information, attendance, school updates, therapy information and records without hunting across disconnected channels.

DESIGN RESPONSEOne parent dashboard + a persistent “My Child” record.
F02
Daily visibility matters more than administrative complexity.

The highest-value information is immediate: attendance, today's report, homework, appointments and important messages.

DESIGN RESPONSEDashboard hierarchy prioritises “what changed today?”
F03
Care coordination crosses school and health contexts.

Therapy, medical appointments, progress information and documents belong in the same care journey rather than separate silos.

DESIGN RESPONSECare, appointments, records and documents share one navigation system.
F04
Access to help should be actionable, not informational.

Parents may need nearby hospitals, child specialists, therapy, pharmacies, ambulance information and government support quickly — especially when navigating ongoing developmental, rehabilitation or medical needs.

DESIGN RESPONSEMedical Help Near Me + low-cost/government-support pathways.
F05
Different people need different access.

A parent, therapist and institute administrator should not see or control the same information.

DESIGN RESPONSERole detection after authentication: Parent · Staff · Institute Admin.
F06
Trust depends on transparency.

Parents need to know who supports their child, what happened that day, what is scheduled next, and where important school, therapy and medical records live.

DESIGN RESPONSEStaff contacts, structured updates, schedule, records and communication history.

LOW-COST CARE DISCOVERY

“Cheapest medical help” became a real search system — not a static directory.

ANTAR’s Medical Help Near Me is designed around the real care-search burden faced by parents of children with special needs. It uses location-aware search to surface relevant healthcare, therapy and support options, then ranks lower-cost/public options ahead of private care when that information can be inferred responsibly.

NeedsHospitals & emergency · child specialists · speech/OT/PT · psychology & behaviour · audiology · diagnostics · pharmacy · assistive devices.
Cost logicGovernment/public facilities rank first, likely-public options next, unknown-cost providers after that, and private providers last.
LocationUse current location or search another locality/PIN; choose a 2, 5, 10, 20 or 30 km radius.
ActionCall, open directions, view details and save useful options rather than only reading a list.
Government supportUDID/disability certificate · RBSK/early intervention · ADIP assistive devices · Niramaya insurance · inclusive education · state/UT schemes.
SafetyCost is presented as public/likely low-cost/private/unknown rather than inventing fees the source does not publish.

ACTUAL FIGMA INTERFACE / NOVA
Research-led design logic

Behavioural insight → product response

N01
Priority is often unclear when everything looks urgent.

The interface needs to make “what matters now” legible without forcing the user to manually compare every task.

FEATUREToday dashboard + priority detail + AI suggestion.
N02
Plans break when the day changes.

A useful planner should help a user understand what a new meeting or deadline affects instead of silently reshuffling the schedule.

FEATUREChange Impact + Adaptive Replan with explanation.
N03
Focus requires protection from context switching.

During deep work, secondary information should disappear rather than compete for attention.

FEATUREDedicated Focus Mode with one task and one next step.
N04
AI needs visible reasoning and user control.

Recommendations become more trustworthy when the user can see why the system suggested something and choose whether to accept it.

FEATURE“Why this matters?” rationale + accept/adjust controls.
N05
Planning lives across different mental models.

Some users think in time, some in projects, some in tasks and some in incoming commitments.

FEATURECalendar · Projects · Tasks · Inbox · Search connected by one system.
N06
Automation should remain adjustable.

The system should support different comfort levels with AI rather than assume full automation is always desirable.

FEATURERecommendation behaviour + automation-level settings.

Autism-adapted learning

A stakeholder-informed comparative study of conventional and autism-adapted web learning interfaces.

Visit site ↗ GitHub ↗
CORE RESEARCH QUESTION

Can an autism-adapted educational interface improve independent task completion and perceived usability without changing the learning task itself?

Phase 1Literature review + needs assessment + stakeholder input
Phase 2Requirements synthesis + adapted interface development
Phase 3Controlled comparison of two interface conditions
MeasuresCompletion · time · errors · assistance · usability · gaze
PUBLICATION PATHWAY
Research publication in progress

The work is structured as two connected papers: Paper 1 — needs assessment + interface development, followed by Paper 2 — controlled comparative evaluation of the autism-adapted and conventional interfaces.

FIELDWORK EVIDENCE / SOREM

Needs assessment before interface refinement.

Phase 1 focused on how professionals supporting autistic learners describe sensory, visual, navigation, communication, interaction and personalisation requirements in digital learning environments.

What I looked forWhere learners lose the next action, what competes for attention, what becomes confusing, and when adult support is needed.
Why it matteredThe goal was to convert observations into traceable UI requirements rather than use a generic “autism-friendly” aesthetic.
Field proofSigned and stamped SOREM acknowledgement documenting the research visit and needs-assessment activity.
Findings → requirements

The recurring needs became controlled design changes.

A01
Predictability reduces interaction uncertainty.

Users should not have to relearn button placement or screen structure on every step.

UI REQUIREMENTStable navigation, repeated control placement and explicit next steps.
A02
Visual calm helps protect task relevance.

Decorative elements can compete with the learning task when visual density is too high.

UI REQUIREMENTMuted palette, reduced decoration and clearly separated task regions.
A03
Hierarchy must make the current task obvious.

The interface should quickly answer: where am I, what am I doing and what happens next?

UI REQUIREMENTStrong title hierarchy, progress orientation and one dominant next action.
A04
User control matters more than visual novelty.

Unexpected motion or ambiguous feedback can add unnecessary interaction burden.

UI REQUIREMENTMinimal motion, literal instructions and immediate non-startling feedback.
A05
Accessibility needs structure, not simplification alone.

Reducing content is not enough; the learner needs content to be sequenced and grouped meaningfully.

UI REQUIREMENTSequential chunking, generous spacing and explicit progress.
A06
A fair study requires a credible control.

The conventional condition must not be intentionally poor. Otherwise the study compares a good interface with a bad one rather than isolates the adaptation.

EXPERIMENTAL RULESame content, questions, scoring, functions and task sequence in both conditions.

HOW THIS SHAPED MY UX RESEARCH APPROACH

This internship is one of the reasons I now begin product work with needs assessment. In my current accessibility and UX work, I carry that same approach forward — study the environment first, identify the real barriers, then translate the evidence into design requirements.

WHY I DO THIS

I want research to make technology easier to live with.

I am especially passionate about accessibility for people with special needs. I want to work on products where research is not just a presentation slide — it changes the interface, the service and the everyday experience of the person using it.

What motivates me most is finding the small barriers that are easy to overlook: confusing navigation, sensory overload, missing information, dependence on another person, or a system that simply was not designed around the user's reality. My goal is to turn those observations into evidence, insights and design decisions that genuinely improve access and independence.
